Dip-rebound research

Can you make money buying large US stocks right after they drop? I tested it four ways on 22 years of daily prices. The answer is no, not after trading costs. Each step below explains what was tested, how, and what came out, in plain words. Click a step to jump to it.

Words used on this page

Edge
How much better a signal did than other stocks bought the same morning and sold the same day. It strips out the whole market going up or down, so what is left is what the signal itself added.
t-stat
How sure we can be that an edge isn't luck. Around 2 or more is the usual bar. Below 1 means it is indistinguishable from chance.
In-sample and out-of-sample
Rules were picked using 2005–2015 data only, then checked on 2016–2026. A real pattern works in both; a lucky one fades.
New stocks (holdout)
835 US stocks over $2B that are not in the S&P 500 or 400. None of them were used to find any rule, so they are a fresh test.
bps (basis points)
Hundredths of a percent. 5 bps per side means losing 0.05% when you buy and again when you sell, from the bid-ask spread and slippage.
CAGR, Sharpe, max fall
Average yearly return; return per unit of risk (higher is better, SPY is about 0.6); and the worst drop from a peak.
Test 1 of 4

Buy every stock that drops 2% in a day

The idea

Your evening prompt looks at big stocks that fell more than 2% on heavy volume and asks whether they bounce. Before adding any AI research, does the drop by itself predict a bounce?

How I tested it

Every S&P 500 and 400 stock from 2005 to October 2026. Signal: down 2% or more, at least 1 million shares traded, price over $20. Buy at the next morning's open, sell 1 to 5 days later.

Test 3 of 4

Try every drop size and definition

The idea

Maybe 2% was simply the wrong number. A 2% drop is routine for a chip stock and rare for a utility, and a slide over several days might matter more than one bad day.

How I tested it

Five ways to measure a drop, 34 cutoffs, holds of 1 to 10 days and three volume filters, on three samples. To count as robust, a setting had to point the same way in all three, be clearly non-random in two, and beat trading costs in all three.

What I found

One-day drops show nothing at any size. Drops over 3 to 5 days do bounce a little, consistently, in all three samples. That is the known short-term reversal effect. It is just smaller than the cost of trading it.

Verdict: real, but too small

No drop size works for one-day drops. Multi-day pullbacks carry a small, genuine bounce, but trading it every day costs about as much as it earns.

Test 4 of 4

Trade the pullback once a week to cut costs

The idea

If costs are the problem, trade less. Rebalance once a week, hold only the 10, 20 or 50 deepest pullbacks, and keep names that still qualify instead of selling and rebuying them.

How I tested it

36 versions, saved to the repository before running. To pass, a version had to beat SPY on both yearly return and Sharpe, at 5 bps per side, in all three samples.

Summary

What this means

Lessons

  • Raw returns mislead. Measure a signal against other stocks bought the same day, or market bounces look like skill.
  • Search enough rules and one will look great by chance. Only a test on data the rule has never seen tells you if it is real.
  • Trading costs decide short-term strategies. A real edge of 0.1% per trade disappears at 5 bps per side.
  • For the evening prompt, the price drop itself gives the AI nothing to build on. Any edge has to come from its news research.

What to try next

  • Measure the AI scores: log each night's scores and check whether high-scored stocks beat low-scored ones the next day, with the same edge measure.
  • Test slower strategies, such as monthly momentum or a 200-day trend filter. They trade about 12 times a year, so costs barely matter.

How the tests were run

  • Daily prices from Yahoo Finance, adjusted for splits and dividends. S&P stocks count only from the date they joined an index, where known.
  • Every trade buys at the next morning's open, the earliest an evening workflow can act.
  • Tests 2 and 4 had their rules and pass marks committed to the repository before the data was run.
  • Stocks that went bankrupt or were bought out are missing from the data. That flatters every result, so real numbers would likely be a bit worse.
